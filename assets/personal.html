Project Biohó · a self-hosted palenque

This page is not
in the cloud.

It is being served to you, right now, by four ESP32-S3 microcontroller blades, sitting on a shelf in a flat in Berlin. They are watched over by two more observer units that witness and visualize the swarm.

The router forwards every request to one immortal virtual MAC address: the crown, worn by whichever blade won the election. The wearer hands your connection to the least busy blade, so you are answered by a different microcontroller from the one you connected to.

Every blade carries both programs, balancer and server, and the crown moves between them by election, held over encrypted radio, off the wire.

The page you are reading lives in flash memory, brotli-compressed, behind a TLS 1.3 handshake that terminates on a 240 MHz microcontroller.

No AWS. No corporate landlord. Absolute autonomy.

The names come from the first free town in the Americas: the capitán is the elected leader, wears the crown and hands every arriving connection to somebody else, while each cimarrón does the actual work of answering it. Every serving blade carries both programs, so which one it is doing depends only on who currently holds the address. The vigía is the watcher that guards the perimeter and keeps the record, and the cabildo is named for the traditional town council - here, the big screen that keeps the settlement's chronicle.

Since the fleet started balancing, the elected node stops serving pages itself: it holds the crown, takes every incoming connection, and splices it to a cimarrón that does the TLS work. It works at layer 4, TCP, and never sees where one request ends and the next begins, so it counts connections while the blades count requests - those are different numbers, since your browser pulls this page, its favicon, the og image and every roster poll down a single connection it keeps open. The blades' figures in the table are requests; the capitán's is their sum.

Connections received is what arrived at the door since this node was elected, relayed is what reached a clean finish, and connections refused is what was turned away instantly because every backend was already busy. Broken mid-transfer is a connection that died in flight, usually a reader closing the tab; closed after stalling is one that went silent and was cleaned up rather than left squatting a slot. In the second row, connect to backend is how long the balancer waits to reach a cimarrón, and time to TLS first byte is how long until that cimarrón sends its first TLS byte back. The response times come from the blades instead - each cimarrón times every request it answers and reports its own p50 and p95, and these are the average across the serving ones.

Architecture

A free settlement, in miniature

The trick that makes a swarm of disposable microcontrollers behave like one durable server is that the identity is not attached to any of them. The blade holding the identity acts as a Level 4 TCP splicer, relaying your connection to one of the downstream serving blades. The fleet behaves like a single machine, but the work is spread across all of them.

The machine itself. Four serving blades on their pads, each with its own jack into the six-port fabric. The small screen is the vigía keeping the roster; the 4.3 inch touch panel is the cabildo keeping the chronicle. Along the front edge runs an SK6812 rail with one pixel per member: gold for the capitán, faint green for the cimarrones, deepest blue for the vigía, deepest purple for the cabildo, red for the lost. Your request enters at the switch, rides the gold cable up to the crown holder, and is spliced back down the green one to the cimarrón that answers it.

The crown. The address you connect to is a locally administered MAC that no factory ever issued. The elected leader writes it into its own Ethernet controller and claims the static IP. Lose that node and the next one in line writes the same address into itself, sends a gratuitous ARP so the switch relearns the port, and carries on answering. The visitor never learns that anything died.

One request, end to end. The capitán splices the raw bytes to the least busy cimarrón, so the encrypted session runs from your browser straight past the balancer. The backend set comes from the radio - every blade broadcasts its state once a second.

The ladder down. A blade is declared lost 3 seconds after its last heartbeat; the next in line wins the election, takes the crown and rebuilds its backend set from the gossip roster. A leader that stands down deliberately closes its relays first; one that dies takes its relays with it. The last blade standing splices to its own server over loopback.

Resilience

The swarm heals itself

Each blade watches its own health, probing its own public port every 7 seconds. The blade that balances watches twice: itself the same way, and the whole fleet through every relay it opens. Either signal can pull a blade out of rotation.

Two health checks. Each blade opens a TCP connection to its own public port every 7 seconds: two failures and it stands down, six and it crashes on purpose so the coredump shows where its server was stuck. The balancer separately benches any backend that accepts connections and returns nothing three times running - 15 seconds, doubling to a 4 minute cap. One byte back clears it.

On the glass

The swarm watches itself

Two touchscreens and a strip of light, all fed by the same 1 Hz heartbeat the blades already send each other. No polling, no server, no dashboard in a browser somewhere - the instruments are part of the machine.

The Vigía’s dashboard

2.8″ TFT · the chronicler

Identity, the vMAC, every member with signal strength, delivery ratio and uptime, then quorum, request count, latency and average RSSI. The six chips along the bottom mirror the physical rail, pixel for pixel.

The rail tells the truth

SK6812 RGBW · six pixels

Gold follows the crown wherever the election moves it. Pull a blade’s power and its pixel turns red inside three seconds. A node that is alive but not yet listening blinks white fast; push a release and it blinks white slowly for the length of the transfer; a blade the balancer has benched holds a steady white.

Boot walks four dots - white, green, blue, red - proving every joint is functional before the roster paints.

The Cabildo’s panel

4.3″ touch · keeps the chronicle

A wider glass for wider truth: health badge, live release progress, sixty-sample sparklines per node and a scrolling chronicle of everything the swarm has done.

It also renders this very page, baked to a pixel strip and scrolled by finger.

Custom setup

One hundred and twenty-three joints

Built as a custom setup, by an electronics technician. Four soldered wires on each blade, both ends, and the unused pins stay bare so the plate sits flat. Joints are metered before they are given power.

01

Wires, not headers

Four blades. On each one, four tails from the USB-C breakout: VBUS to 5V-IN, GND to GND, D+ to pin 20, D− to pin 19. No pin-header strips. No Duponts between the breakout and the blade. Every other pin stays empty.

02

The same port flashes the blade

Those tails are also the ESP32-S3’s native USB. Flash by pulling that blade’s lead off the fuse board and plugging the laptop into the same breakout. One breakout’s silk swaps D+ and D−, so each pair is metered before it sees a computer.

03

Six ports off one brick

A GST60A feeds the stripboard. Six USB-C sockets leave it: four blades, the vigía, and the cabildo. The two lid fans leave through 2-pin screw terminals, and those branches are fused too. The fans are not fitted. Forty-seven joints on that board: 12 on the USB-C ports, 12 holding those ports to the board, 16 on the fuses, 3 on the barrel jack, and 4 on the two screw terminals.

04

The screens, plugged not soldered

A rainbow of female-to-female Duponts carries SPI, backlight and touch from the DevKitC to the 2.8″ glass. Nothing on a display is permanent: every link pulls apart, so replacing a dead panel needs no soldering iron.

05

The shifter

The vigía speaks 3.3 volts. The LEDs listen for five. A 74AHCT125 repeats the signal at 5 V, and a 330 Ω resistor softens the edge. The wires beyond that board stay push-fit. This is GPIO level on the display board, not how the blades talk to each other. They take 5 V in, and an onboard regulator makes their 3.3 V.

06

Measured, then powered

Continuity first, then volts. No rail shorted to ground. Five volts where five volts belong. Then the lead is plugged in.

07

One bohío for the four

Next, the naked boards get a floor. The four cimarrones share one 3D-printed plate, the bohío: a nest for each blade and a well for its breakout, taped to the top of the switch. RJ45 noses face the rear.

08

The shell is the rack

One printed shell holds the whole machine: the switch, the power brick, the fuse board, the bohío, and both screens. The cimarrones sit side by side, jacks aft, and the screens face forward through the front wall.

Principles

Rules the swarm lives by

Wire silence

Only the node wearing the crown answers the world. The other blades receive their visitors through it over the LAN and send nothing outward on their own, so from outside the swarm looks like a single quiet machine.

Crash only

Nothing shuts down gracefully because nothing needs to. There is no state worth preserving on any node. Pulling the plug is a supported operation and the only recovery procedure is to boot.

Own the identity

The address is a locally administered MAC chosen by hand and passed between blades by election. No provider issued it and none can revoke it.

One resource

The entire site is a single brotli-compressed HTML file with no external fonts, scripts, trackers or CDNs. A first-time visitor pays for one TLS handshake and one response.

Deterministic election

The lowest numbered candidate that is healthy both on radio and network leads. Every node computes the same answer from the same heartbeats, making leader election deterministic.

Reachable forever

Every blade boots into its own identity before it is eligible to lead, so a bad release can always be replaced remotely. The swarm can be rebuilt over the air without anyone touching a cable.

Prove you can serve

Every blade routinely opens a TCP connection to probe its own public port. Two failures and it stands down from the election; six and it reboots. A blade that cannot reach itself has no business receiving visitors.

Judge by outcomes

The balancer ignores what a blade claims about itself. A backend that goes silent on three relays in a row is benched for 15 seconds, and each repeat doubles the bench, up to four minutes.

Degrade to solo

Lose the leader and the next blade becomes one in seconds. Keep losing blades and the backend set shrinks with the fleet, down to the last survivor splicing to its own server over loopback.
